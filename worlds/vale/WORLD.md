# WENNOW VALE

> The first fantastic world. It answers to [PHILOSOPHY.md](../../PHILOSOPHY.md) in
> everything; where this document and that one disagree, that one wins.

The philosophy told a fable about a second mapmaker who planted a pear tree
where its blossoms would show over the hill. It mentioned, in passing, a
lighthouse that blinks in sevens, a place called Wennow Cross, standing
stones named after a grandmother's cats, a ferryman who eats at noon, a
river that keeps a secret, and seven chimneys over six hearths.

Those weren't examples. That was the world, announcing itself. Our first
world is **the second mapmaker's valley** — we are building the fable's own
proof.

**Wennow Vale**: one sea-valley, one river, one road that climbs. Small
enough to hold in the heart, dense enough to never finish. The player knows
it as "somewhere" until the world tells them its name (see *The title that
learns*, below).

Companion documents: **[COLOR.md](COLOR.md)** — the color
script (where the vale is quiet and where it detonates);
**[VOICES.md](VOICES.md)** — the voice bible (how people
here actually talk); **[LEDGER.md](LEDGER.md)** — the
mysteries, audited.

## 1. The shape of the vale

The river **Wennow** rises on the high moor and runs south to the sea. The
vale is its valley: the harbor at its mouth, the village at its ford, the
wood on its west bank, the moor above the wood, the dye terraces on its east
bank, the locks where it narrows, the gorge stair to its head. One vale. The
sea at the bottom of the world, the unmapped at the top.

```
            THE HEAD ─ Maren's hut · the doorframe · the Page
               │
            THE STEPS ─ gorge stair · hanging orchard · her bench
               │
   THE NINE CATS ──┐
   (moor, stones,  │
    the Under,     ├ THE LOCKS ─ weirs · eel traps · Sela's cottage
    the Glimmer) ──┤     │
               │   │  THE DYE YARDS ─ pigment terraces · Nettle's lines
    THE HUSHES ────┤     │
    (the wood,     │     │
     the Hollow) ──┴──┬──┘
                      │
            PEAR RIDGE ─ the Bend · the tree · Fen's watch · overlook
               │
          WENNOW CROSS ─ the ford · seven chimneys · the bell · the Rose
               │
            SALTMOUTH ─ the Landing · Gull Head · the lighthouse
               │
             THE SEA
```

**Why a river valley:** rivers organize worlds honestly. Water always runs
somewhere, so a lost player who follows any stream is delivered, not
rescued — the Law of Gentle Ground built into the geology. A coast gives us
arrival by water, a lighthouse, and a horizon. A valley head gives the world
its lean: everything in the vale gently faces uphill, toward the one place
the maps stop.

**Scale:** walking the spine without stopping, Landing to Head, takes about
eight minutes. Nobody will ever do that. Target: three to five unhurried
hours before a player *feels* done; the vale never says so. Height is real:
sea level to high moor, and the overlooks cash the climb.

**Distances are measured in bells** (the time between bell-hours):
"the Cross is half a bell from the Landing." No meters, no minutes. The
milestone on the ridge is carved `WENNOW CROSS ½`.

**There is no money in the vale.** Customs instead: the traveler's loaf is
put out each morning at the bakery ("custom"), and leaving a pear on a
doorstep is a greeting. This is charm, and it spares us ever building an
economy.

**The player carries three things into the vale:** a journal (blank but for
the pasted ferry ticket), an undyed wool scarf, and time. Everything else is
found, borrowed, carried in the hands, or learned.

## 2. The quiet throughline: Maren

The second mapmaker has a name: **Maren**. She grew up at the Cross, in her
grandmother's cottage — the grandmother whose cats named the standing
stones. She mapped the vale her whole life, and every map she drew has one
edge left deliberately blank; she said a finished map is a lie about the
world.

Years ago she walked up past the Steps to sketch "the next page," and she
has been up there a while now. Not lost, not tragic — *working*. Pages
still come down the vale: she drops corrections in oilcloth into the river,
and Sela finds them in the eel baskets. ("Got one Tuesday. The alder's
fallen, apparently.") And her orders still come down too: Nettle at the Dye
Yards steeps the inks Maren asks for, and you can tell what country she's
drawing by what colors she wants. *She's on umber now. Was years of green.*

**Her survey line is the world's spine gesture.** Maren paid herself in
pear trees — she planted one at every station of her survey, Landing to
Head. The blossom line IS her route. The petals that ride the evening wind
up the vale are the world's one long arrow, and it is made of orchard, not
interface.

The player is never told any of this, and there is no "find Maren"
anywhere. The vale just leans upward, and everything of hers points on up.

**The last page (the ending — such as it is):** at the head of the vale
stands her hut: warm, kettle on, a cat that seems to have been waiting.
On the table, her newest map — of THIS vale, finished except one edge, and
drawn onto it, small, is a figure on the paths the player actually walked
most. Her map has been recording *you*. The world explored back.

Past the hut, a doorframe stands alone in the grass (walk through it or
around it; same thing; charm). Beyond, the next valley exists only as
pencil sketch — and it inks itself in, in color, with every step you take
into it. Far off, always one hill further, a small figure straightens up,
and waves. Once. You never quite reach her. You were never meant to. The
journal's last sketch is the wave.

You can walk back down after. Dusk falls; the vale's lights come on below;
you live there now, if you like. Done is a feeling.

## 3. The regions

Each place below carries its **gestures** (how it asks to be found — Law 2)
and its **whys** (Law 8). Mysteries are marked ◆ and live in
[LEDGER.md](LEDGER.md). Color keys per region are scored in
[COLOR.md](COLOR.md).

### 3.1 Saltmouth — the mouth of the world

Where the player arrives and the sea ends the map. Harbor, headland,
lighthouse. *Color: Key of Milk — bleached blues, gull-white, apricot
dawns; prism glints in the pools.*

- **The Landing.** Ferry berth, a bench, tide marks on the piles. The game
  opens mid-crossing at morning: gulls, the vale unfolding from the water,
  the lighthouse blinking (countable from minute one). The ferryman says
  almost all he ever says: *"Mind the step. Noon run's from the bell."*
  No goal is issued. None ever will be.
- **The lighthouse on Gull Head.** Door ajar when Iris is in; a spiral
  stair; the lamp room you may stand in. ◆ It blinks in sevens, and always
  has.
- **The Good Rock.** The herons contest it every morning against the gulls.
  Sitting nearby long enough is how you learn who's winning lately. Feed
  the herons often enough and they take to escorting your ferry rides —
  two grey outriders, low over the water, unexplained to other passengers.
- **The tide pools.** Restocked twice a day by the tide: shells, sea glass
  throwing small prisms at low sun, ◆ occasionally a marble.
- **The boathouse.** An upturned hull, patched — look close — with pieces
  of an old map. ◆ The vale, drawn young. First Maren-mark, five steps off
  the first jetty, for whoever leans in.
- **Doings here:** skim stones off the jetty (Odd rates every throw,
  deadpan, without being asked: *"Seven. Tide helped."*); ride the ferry
  for the ride; stand the **fog watch** if Odd ever asks you (see Asks).

*Gestures:* the lamp at night; herons to the Rock at dawn; smoke above the
Cross showing over the first hill; petals blown along the coast road. The
first five minutes carry the densest guidance budget in the vale, and none
of it is UI.

### 3.2 Wennow Cross — the village at the ford

Crossroads of the coast road, the ridge road, and the mill lane; stepping
stones over the Wennow. *Color: warm naturals, and every front door painted
a color somebody once argued for — the vale's palette in miniature, one
door per story.*

- **Seven chimneys, six hearths.** Count them. The seventh chimney stands
  alone in the yew hedge, belonging to no house, ◆ warm to the touch at
  dusk, with a little iron soot-door that has no keyhole. It never opens.
  This is the vale's unopenable door (Law 9), and it is load-bearing.
  (Grim, the laziest cat in the vale, sleeps on top of it. Make of that
  what you will. The ledger has.)
- **The ford.** Stepping stones; on the third one, carved small and old:
  ◆ `M + T`. Paddling allowed. Encouraged, honestly.
- **The bakery.** Perl bakes before dawn — her smoke is the vale's first
  gesture every day. The traveler's loaf is put out on the sill. The
  bakery cat sleeps in the proof baskets and is not to be moved.
- **The bell.** Rung on the hour by whoever's nearest — including you.
  That's the custom, and it has teeth: ring it wrong (thirteen, once,
  famously — see Customs) and the vale runs on your mistake all day.
- **The chapel and the Sea-Glass Rose.** Tiny; a ship model hangs in it;
  the chapel cat attends services and sits at the front. The window above
  the altar is the **Rose**: generations of fishing families leaded it
  from wreck-glass and bottle-bottoms, no two panes alike, each pane a
  boat — the chapel book says which is whose. On clear days around noon
  the sun comes through it and the whole nave goes kaleidoscopic: colored
  light lying on the floor, the pews, the cat (this is why the cat
  attends), and you, if you stand in it. Stand in it. The journal sketches
  you in color for the first time.
  ◆ The chapel doorstep is a single long stone, oddly shaped, worn smooth
  (see the Ledger: the Tenth Cat).
- **The notice board.** Diegetic text only: lost gloves, ferry times
  ("ish"), *"petals going up again,"* a child's drawing pinned crooked, a
  color-order slip in a fast hand: *"orchil, two bottles, when convenient
  — M."* Rumors live here; objectives never will.
- **Grandmother's cottage.** Ada's now. She feeds the vale's cats at dusk
  and knows every stone's cat-name; she'll tell one per conversation,
  never the same twice, ◆ and across enough evenings she names *ten*.
- **Little Wick.** A child who chalks maps on the flagstones — you can
  chalk with her; your drawings stay until the rain, and villagers step
  around them and comment. Her maps are wrong in interesting ways —
  except ◆ one shows a pond in the Hushes that no adult remembers. She
  owns a brass-and-tin kaleidoscope she calls **the Looker**, and there is
  exactly one way to earn a lifetime lend of it (see Asks).

*Gestures:* the bell; chalk maps; lit windows telling the truth at night;
petals in the gutter going the wrong way; on clear days, colored light
spilling out the chapel door onto the step, like the building is leaking.

### 3.3 Pear Ridge — the road that shows you the world

The ridge road climbs out of the Cross; at the Bend stands the pear tree of
the fable — blossom showing over the hill exactly as promised. *Color: airy
green-gold, blossom act one.*

- **The tree at the Bend.** A bench beneath it. Windfall pears you may
  carry — one at a time, in your hands — and give to anyone. Everyone
  responds in their own key (Odd salutes with his; Ada slices hers for the
  cats; Fen goes quiet: *"she used to bring me these."*).
- **The Overlook.** At the Bend the camera breathes out and the whole lower
  vale is suddenly below — tiny and alive: the ferry mid-crossing if it's
  crossing time, smoke, herons, dye-cloth rippling color on the east bank,
  the sea. The Law of the Horizon delivered mechanically: everything you
  can see down there, you can walk to. First wonder, seven minutes in.
- **Fen's watch.** A telescope on a tripod, a kettle, slate after slate of
  tally marks. Fen is the traveler from the fable — still up here, years
  in, counting the lighthouse. Perfectly happy. You may aim the telescope
  yourself, freely: framed true views of the lamp room, the stones on the
  moor, the Steps far up the vale, whatever you find.
- **The milestone.** `WENNOW CROSS ½ · ACROSS —` ◆ the distance to Across
  has been chiselled out. By someone. At some point.
- **Doings here:** the long grass slope below the Bend is the vale's best
  **slide** — sit, tip forward, and the hill does the rest, petals
  streaming past your ears.

### 3.4 The Hushes — where lost is a place

The wood on the west bank. Paths braid and the canopy closes; the camera
tightens (cozy, never grim); the deeper in, the quieter, until your own
steps are the loudest thing — and then you hear the newts plip, and follow
that. *Color: deep greens, until the Hollow, where it detonates pastel.*

- **The Chalk Pond.** Little Wick's pond: lilies, newts, and a very good
  echo. Sitting here writes birdsong into the journal, in notation.
- **The Foxglove Hollow.** A hidden basin where the wood stops holding
  itself in: foxgloves, campion, and cow-parsley riot floor-to-canopy —
  pink, lavender, cream, butter — pollen motes burning gold in the light
  shafts, and the bees audibly drunk. The vale's first full pastel
  explosion, kept inside a plain green wood the way the vale keeps
  everything: so that walking into it feels like a held breath let go.
  (Why: a spring under the basin keeps it sweet; the old dyers seeded it
  generations back and let it run wild. The Hollow is where Nettle picks
  her pinks. She'll say so, eventually.)
- **The Green Chapel.** A mossy stone arch older than the chapel in the
  village; birdsong converges on it. Nobody built it recently.
- **The charcoal ring.** Warm ash some mornings. ◆ Somebody burns here by
  night. (Perl's shy brother Hob — the player only ever catches the
  *leaving* of him. The world runs unwatched.)
- **The rule that keeps lost gentle:** every stream in the Hushes reaches
  the Wennow; light gaps always mark the edges; fireflies at night make
  slow mint-gold lanes of it. You cannot be trapped here, only pleasantly
  misplaced.

### 3.5 The Nine Cats — the stones on the moor

Open moor above the wood; wind always; every bush leans up-valley (the
wind is a signpost that doesn't know it). *Color: heather purple — at
sunset the whole moor burns violet-rose, the vale's biggest cheap
firework, free every clear evening.*

- **The stones.** Nine, standing in rough court. At the base of each,
  carved small: a cat's name — Moss, Tansy, Grim, Puck, Fern, Soot, Dab,
  Whisk — and a tenth hollow in the heather, socket-shaped, empty. ◆ Ada
  names ten cats. The tenth stone is the chapel doorstep at the Cross,
  moved "for luck" the year the bell cracked — lifting it is what cracked
  the bell. Its worn carving reads `OLD NIM`. Solving this rewards no
  thing at all: it re-lights four other details you've already seen. That
  is the model mystery of the vale.
- **Finding all nine names:** nothing is awarded. The journal quietly draws
  the cats, curled together, on one page. Curiosity's wage, paid in world.
- **The Under, and the Glimmer.** A fold in the moor you can slip down
  into — falling in IS the discovery (Law 7). First the long dark: your
  steps, a black stream, glowworms coming on one by one until the dark is
  a slow constellation. Then the chamber the locals call **the Glimmer**:
  a seam of spar crystal over a little fall. Most hours it is a cave with
  good acoustics. But at clear-day noon, one sun-shaft finds the seam —
  and the chamber detonates: split light walking every wall, standing
  rainbows in the fall's mist, the black stream running spectral. Timing
  it is knowledge (noon; clear; the Law of the Only Key), and the long
  dark before it is the withholding that makes it land. The passage comes
  out, blinking, at the Locks — a shortcut earned by daring, our fast
  travel.

### 3.6 The Dye Yards — where the vale keeps its color

The east bank below the Locks: pigment terraces stepped down the hillside,
pool after pool of steeped color — madder rose, woad blue, weld yellow,
walnut umber, orchil violet — and long lines of drying cloth rippling in
the wind like a hillside dressed in flags. The vale's daytime color
detonation, and the answer to a question a mapmaker's valley had to
answer: *where did her inks come from?* Here. *Color: Key of the Fair —
the one place the vale goes saturated, and it earns it.*

- **Nettle.** The dyer. Ageless in the way of people who work outdoors,
  arms stained to the elbow in every color she's ever made. Still steeps
  the pools because Maren still orders inks — the slips come down with
  the eel-basket pages — and because the pools would sulk otherwise. She
  speaks in colors the way Odd speaks in weather.
- **The butterflies.** They mob the yards, and they prefer the weld-yellow
  pool. ◆ Nobody knows why. Nettle has asked them.
- **Doings here:** stir a vat (it wants stirring; Nettle will point with
  her chin); **dip your scarf** — one pool, one stripe, layering over
  visits, so your scarf slowly becomes a record of your own colors in
  your own order. It is the game's only "customization," and it is made
  entirely of places you've been. ◆ Rarely, a dip comes out a color that
  is in no pool. *"Wasn't me. Pool did that on its own."*
- **When rain comes** (the world's weather, not a script), the cloth has
  to come in, and one pair of hands is never enough. Be nearby and
  you're in it — armfuls of color, wind fighting you for every sheet,
  Nettle laughing at the sky. Nobody calls this a quest. It's weather.

*Gestures:* the cloth lines are visible from the Overlook and from the
ridge road — a flag of every color, waving at the whole vale, which is
how the vale tells you the yards exist.

### 3.7 The Locks — where the river is worked

The Wennow narrows; old weirs and lock gates; eel traps; a cottage with
laundry out. *Color: copper-green waterworks; the eel run is silver
lightning in dark water.*

- **Sela, the keeper.** Winds the weir gates on her own hours. Learn her
  schedule and you can ride the gates upstream — the Only Key: passage
  paid in attention. She finds Maren's pages in the eel baskets and keeps
  them in a box; she'll show you, eventually, if you've been the kind of
  visitor worth showing. (Untracked. She just remembers.) Her eyes aren't
  what they were — which occasions the best quiet hour in the vale (see
  Asks: Reading to Sela).
- **The eel run.** At certain dusks the river briefly boils silver. A
  missable moment; being real is the point.
- **The laundry is a diagram.** ◆ Every evening, Sela's washing lifts and
  points up-valley: the sea breathes in of an evening. This is the answer
  to the vale's most famous small mystery — the petals that float
  UPSTREAM at dusk — hiding in plain sight on a clothesline.
- **The winch.** ◆ The weir has been "wrong since the flood took the good
  winch," and Sela can go on about the winch at length. You can get her
  started on the winch. People do, at gatherings, on purpose.

### 3.8 The Steps — her stair through the gorge

Maren's survey stair, cut into the gorge side, pear trees rooted in the
ledges (the hanging orchard — the petal supply). *Color: blossom act two,
fortissimo — petal-fall like warm pink-cream snow with the evening light
coming through it sideways.*

- Her slate way-marks survive, and they are not directions. They are
  observations: *"kestrel nests here in May."* *"Echo answers twice."*
  Every one is TRUE, and testing them is the game honoring its own
  details. ◆ The echo really answers twice. No one will ever explain the
  second one.
- **The echo game.** The echo returns what you hum, doubled. You can play
  it like an instrument — call phrases, get them back braided — and if
  you've been listening all game, you can teach it the vale's tune,
  phrase by phrase, and stand in the gorge while it sings both parts.
  Nothing unlocks. It's just the best sound in the game.
- **The falls.** On misted full-moon nights, a ◆ moonbow stands in the
  spray — pale, complete, gone by morning. One of her way-marks says
  simply: *"moon + mist = yes."*
- **Her bench.** The last bench before the top. From it, the whole vale —
  which by now means: your whole journey, visible at once. Here, and only
  when opened, the journal renders its map: everywhere you have actually
  walked, in your own line. The first map the game ever shows is one the
  player drew with their feet.

### 3.9 The Head — the hut, the doorframe, the Page

Described in §2. The warmest room in the game and the widest silence.
Beyond the doorframe, the sketch-world: the one place the color script
runs backwards — pencil greys that bloom into full color footstep by
footstep, the whole palette the vale taught you arriving at once, with the
tune. The game does not end here; it just stops withholding the fact that
it was never counting.

## 4. No dead ground

The walk between places is also the game. Doctrine for every stretch of
connective tissue in the vale:

- **The wind is one system.** Grass, cloth, petals, smoke, laundry, and
  water chop all obey the same wind — gusts cross the whole vale visibly,
  a silver wave running up a hillside of grass and setting the dye-lines
  rippling two fields away. This single decision does more to make the
  vale feel alive than any crowd of characters.
- **Verges perform.** Drifts of self-sown flowers where the wind piles
  seeds; a stile in every wall, each one built different (whoever built
  it argued with whoever built the last one); cart ruts that remember
  markets; bees with actual routes you can follow to actual flowers.
- **Micro-encounters, unscripted-feeling, schedule-driven:** a pheasant
  detonating out of a hedge (the vale's one jump-scare, forever); the fox
  crossing the ford at night; a dropped glove that belongs to someone
  findable; two cats pretending not to see each other across a lane.
- **Pockets.** Between the named regions, the vale keeps small found
  places that belong to nobody's route: the bramble maze behind the
  chapel (blackberries, and a way through only kids know), the boat
  graveyard on the shingle (ribs of old hulls, each with a name still
  legible), the icehouse ruin (cold air breathing out of a green door),
  the swallow bridge (stand under it at dusk and the birds pour out
  around you), the wishing stump (coins hammered edge-on into old wood,
  a custom nobody starts and nobody stops).
- **The rule:** stand anywhere in the vale for ten seconds and at least
  one thing — light, wind, creature, sound — must be worth the standing.
  If a stretch fails this, it is a bug at a known coordinate. Fix the
  corner, never add a signpost. (This is the philosophy's Rule of Three
  Temptations, Amendment II, applied: every screenful holds at least
  three noticeable things.)

## 5. The people

Ten, plus a cat that waits, a cat that naps, and a figure that waves.
Small on purpose: each person must be knowable the way a neighbor is.
**Their voices are specified — as grammars, with sample lines — in
[VOICES.md](VOICES.md).** Nobody sounds like anybody else,
and nobody sounds like a writer.

| Person | Where | Routine spine | The thing they wonder about |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Odd Bole** | ferry | runs dawn / midmorning / noon / dusk; pie on the bell bench at noon sharp | whether the herons plan it |
| **Iris Kell** | lighthouse | polishes at dusk; lamp at dark | what her mother never told her about the sevens |
| **Perl** | bakery | fires oven before dawn; sill loaf out at first light | where her brother gets to at night (she knows; she likes wondering) |
| **Hob** | the Hushes, at night | burns charcoal; seen only leaving | (never speaks; waves once, like someone else does) |
| **Ada** | grandmother's cottage | feeds cats at dusk, one story per evening | which came first, the villagers' names or the cats' |
| **Little Wick** | the Cross flagstones | chalks after lunch; loses marbles off the jetty | whether the mermaids are collecting them |
| **Fen** | Pear Ridge watch | counts, tallies, kettle always just boiled | eight |
| **Sela** | the Locks | winds gates on her hours; checks eel baskets at dusk | what the alder falling meant to whoever wrote it down |
| **Nettle** | the Dye Yards | steeps by morning, dries by wind, delivers by whim | why the butterflies only love the yellow |
| **Maren** | up | draws | the next page |

**And the chorus.** Beyond the majors, the vale keeps a chorus of minor
folk — the post with the knee, the curate who wept once, the market
granny the pears fear, kids with jars of nearly-fog — each with one great
text-box line, two at most (see [VOICES.md](VOICES.md) §chorus). On
**market mornings** (weekly, the Cross flagstones) and Lantern Nights the
chorus doubles: stalls, dogs, arguments about plums, a fiddle nobody
admits to owning. Density of people is density of world; the knowable
cast stays ten.

**The traveler is silent.** The player speaks in gestures — nod, shake,
shrug, point, show-what's-in-your-hands, small bow — and villagers read
them generously, sometimes too generously, which is a comedy engine and a
kindness at once. Introducing yourself is a gesture too: show someone the
name written on your journal's flyleaf, and your name starts traveling the
vale at gossip speed (see §6). Until then you are "the traveler," then
"the walker," then whatever Perl decides ("the bread-runner"), then —
suddenly, from a stranger — your actual name, and you get to wonder which
route it took.

## 6. The clock, the weather, the life

Per the philosophy: **the world clock is the first system built.**

- **The day** runs ~24 real minutes (tuned in build): dawn → morning →
  NOON BELL → afternoon → dusk → night. Time persists across sessions;
  the vale you return to has gone on without you.
- **Schedules** (the routine lattice — the thing the player slowly learns,
  because knowledge is the only key):
  - *Dawn:* Perl's smoke (the day's first gesture); herons take the Good
    Rock; first ferry; Nettle walks her pools, talking to them a little.
  - *Noon:* the bell; Odd's pie; the chapel cat's rounds; on clear days,
    the Rose fires the nave and the Glimmer fires the cave — the vale's
    two noon secrets, held at opposite ends and both on the clock.
  - *Afternoon:* Sela winds the gates; Wick chalks; cloth comes off the
    lines dry.
  - *Dusk:* Ada feeds the cats; the sea breathes in — petals go upstream;
    the eel run (some evenings); Iris polishes; the lamp lights.
  - *Night:* fireflies in the Hushes; a fox crosses the ford; the lit
    windows tell the truth about who's home; Hob burns.
- **News walks.** Gossip is simulated at walking speed: things you do are
  carried person to person at their actual meetings (Ada tells Perl over
  the morning loaf; Perl tells Odd at the noon pie; Odd tells nobody, but
  Wick was listening). Do something at the Landing at dawn and hear about
  yourself, slightly wrong, at the Locks by dusk. The vale has no feed.
  It has neighbors.
- **Weather:** clear; sea-mist mornings (the vale half-erased — beauty,
  and a new reading of known places); rain (rare — everyone under eaves,
  the streams gain voices, the flagstones turn to mirrors and the vale
  hangs upside-down in them); wind days (the one wind, showing off); and,
  very rarely, unannounced, ◆ the **Green Evening** — ninety seconds of
  gold-green light over everything. Nobody explains it. Fen tallies those
  too.

### Customs (the vale's calendar of small ceremonies)

- **The hour bell** is rung by whoever's nearest, you included. Ring it
  wrong — thirteen, say — and the vale *keeps* your mistake: "the long
  noon," meals late all day, and you hear about it, warmly, for a season.
  Consequences here are social, never punitive.
- **Lantern Night**, each full moon: the vale walks down to the water at
  dusk and floats paper lanterns from the Cross to the sea — a ribbon of
  drifting gold and pastel lights the whole length of the river, Odd out
  on the black water herding strays with an oar, muttering. You can fold
  your own (an actual small folding interaction, learnable by watching),
  write a wish inside that nobody will ever read, and set it going. The
  custom: the eldest lights the first lantern from the bakery oven's
  ember. Ada's knees no longer do the bank (see Asks).
- **The traveler's loaf**, the doorstep pear, and the string tied round a
  promise — see §8.

## 7. The doings

Walking is the game; these decorate walking. None are gated, none are
tracked, none award numbers. They are worth doing because of what they do,
which is the only reason the vale respects.

- **Skim stones** — real physics toy; every throw judged (rated by Odd if
  he's near, imitated by Wick if she is, ignored magnificently by cats).
- **Ring the bell** — if you're nearest at the hour, it's yours to ring.
  A small responsibility the vale hands you without asking your level.
- **Chalk with Wick** — actually draw on the flagstones; it stays until
  rain; the vale steps around your art and reviews it out loud.
- **Dip your scarf** — at the Dye Yards; one pool, one stripe; your color
  history worn on your neck.
- **Play the echo** — hum at the Steps; it answers doubled; teach it the
  tune if you've gathered the phrases with your ears.
- **Press flowers** — any flower you're carrying can go in the journal; it
  dries over real days into the page.
- **Aim the telescope** — Fen's, freely; the vale's far detail up close.
- **Raise the Looker** — Wick's kaleidoscope, once lent: raise it to your
  eye anywhere and the whole world renders through it — six-fold, slowly
  turning, any view a mandala. Pure toy. Zero function. Maximum wage.
- **Sit** — anywhere: benches, steps, the Good Rock's neighbor rock. Time
  gently flows, the camera widens, routines play out. Sitting is how you
  watch the herons lose.
- **Pet the cats** — each has spot preferences and a place they'll allow
  it. The ferry cat, only mid-crossing. Grim, anywhere, obviously,
  he's asleep.
- **Slide** — the grass slope below the Bend, and two more slides the
  vale keeps quiet about.
- **Paddle / float** — the ford, the shallows; deep water floats you
  gently back, gasping happily.
- **Fold a lantern** — Lantern Nights; learned by watching hands, not by
  a prompt.
- **Carry** — one thing at a time, in your hands, offered to anyone or
  set down anywhere. Your hands are the inventory.

## 8. Asks & strings

*(Per PHILOSOPHY.md, Amendment I — the doctrine of asks: people may ask,
face to face, at the right hour; never through an interface.)*

**How it works.** Someone asks you something in their own words — always
refusable with a shake of the head, no penalty, being asked is itself the
vale's compliment. Accept with a nod and a **string** ties itself to your
journal's edge with a tiny sketch of the asker: the vale's custom for
remembering promises. Kept, the string unties itself (small, satisfying).
Forgotten, it just stays — patient, pretty, unscolding. No log, no text,
no tracker, no timer, no reward tab. What kept promises earn is worldly
and relational: trust, doors, names, hours nobody else gets.

**The asks (the "quests," in the only form the vale allows):**

- **The Bread Round.** Perl, mornings, once you've been up-valley:
  *"Loaf's hers. Gets stale or gets carried, no business of mine which."*
  Carry it to Sela. She doesn't say thank you; she says *"You'll want the
  gate in a minute. Hold the middle rope."* — and the ride up is the
  thanks. Teaches the up-valley route, the string custom, and the vale's
  whole theory of gratitude in five minutes.
- **The Marble Trade.** Wick, ford or jetty: bring her ONE marble the sea
  took (tide pools, low tide, patience) and the Looker is yours on
  permanent lend. *"Even trade. The sea doesn't NEED that one."*
- **The Fog Watch.** A fog morning, eventually: Odd asks you to stand at
  the harbor bell and ring the pattern while he brings the ferry in
  blind. You ring; you strain to hear his oar-knocks answer; the fog
  gives you nothing for a long time; then the bow. Afterward he says your
  name. First time. That's the whole reward, and it's enormous.
- **Reading to Sela.** Her eyes aren't what they were. She'll hand you
  Maren's latest page and keep winding while you read it aloud — the
  player reads the actual page, at her pace. She corrects your
  pronunciation of "alder." This is how Maren-lore enters the game: out
  loud, over work, at dusk.
- **Fen's Letter.** He asks you — finally, momentously — to carry a
  letter down to Iris. He catches you before the lighthouse, out of
  breath, and takes it back. He has done this to everyone in the vale.
  Everyone knows. Iris knows. One dusk, without a word about it, she
  walks up the ridge and sits by his telescope, and you learn that some
  asks are kept by failing them far enough.
- **Where's Grim.** Ada, recurring: her laziest cat is "off again."
  Grim sleeps in absurd places — the ferry's rope coil, a proof basket,
  the eel box, on top of the seventh chimney — and finding him is a
  gentle recurring hide-and-seek with a comic journal page growing per
  find. He is never actually lost. Ada knows that. It's a reason to send
  you looking at the vale, and both of you know that too.
- **Ada's Lantern.** Lantern Night: her knees don't do the bank anymore.
  Carry her lantern down and you light the vale's first flame of the
  night from the oven ember, with the whole village watching and nobody
  making a thing of it. The honor is that nobody makes a thing of it.
- **The Rain Rush.** Not asked in words at all: rain coming, cloth on the
  lines, Nettle already running. Be there, grab an armful, fight the wind
  for it. Afterward: *"You're rained on. Good. Means it counted."*

**The rules of asks:** contextual (right person, right hour, right
history); at most a couple live at once; never chained into an arc-with-a-
tracker; never required for anything the vale considers essential; and
every one resolves into story, never into loot. If an ask would feel at
home on a bulletin board with a gold "!" — it doesn't ship.

## 9. The tune

The vale has one melody. Maren hummed it; everyone caught it wrong in
their own way. The world's music system is the tune, assembled by
exploration:

- Perl hums the first phrase, kneading.
- The bell *should* ring its third note but rings it flat (the crack).
- Little Wick chalks its notes on the flags, two of them wrong.
- The echo at the Steps returns any phrase doubled — and can be taught.
- The glowworms in the Under pulse its rhythm, slowed.
- Beyond the doorframe, in the sketch-world, it plays whole for the first
  time — the ending is the tune, finally assembled, which the player has
  been collecting without being told.

Score direction: sparse, diegetic-first (wind, water, bell, birds carry
pitch); the tune surfaces rarely enough to be a sighting.

## 10. The color script

Lives in **[COLOR.md](COLOR.md)** — the vale's palette
dramaturgy: a naturalistic world in the Key of Moss that holds pockets
where color detonates (the Hollow's pastels, the Yards' saturated flags,
the Rose and the Glimmer going full prism at noon, the moor burning violet
at dusk, the sketch-world's finale where color itself is the reward). The
law of it: **color always has a source** — light through glass, spar,
mist, petal, pigment — and saturation is a gesture: a glow over a hill is
a promise the hill keeps.

The script names **ten postcards** — the showpiece renders the vale is
built to produce — and they double as the Postcard Test's standing
targets.

## 13. The title that learns

The player starts the game and the title screen says only, in small type,
**somewhere**. When the vale's name is learned in-world — a sign, a
villager, the milestone — the title screen says **WENNOW VALE** from then
on, in that player's copy, forever. The game's own name is the first
secret it keeps and the first the world gives up. The philosophy asked for
the name to be found the way everything here is found: by going and
looking. So it is.
