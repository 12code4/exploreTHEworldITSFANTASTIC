# If a World Is Fantastic

> **IF A WORLD IS FANTASTIC, IT'S EXPLORED.**

This is the fable the whole game stands on. This document irons it flat: what
the sentence means, what "fantastic" is made of, the laws it forces on us, the
things it forbids, the promises it makes to the player, and the tests that
tell us whether we're keeping them.

Everything else — the world, its name, its code — comes after this file, and
answers to it.

---

## The fable, told properly

Two mapmakers each owned a valley, and each wanted travelers.

The first built signposts at every fork. He painted arrows on the rocks,
numbered the landmarks, and paid a copper coin for each one visited. Travelers
came, followed the arrows, collected the coins, and left. When the coins ran
out, so did the travelers. The arrows still point at the empty valley.

The second spent her years differently. She planted a pear tree at the bend of
the ridge road, where its blossoms would show over the hill and anyone could
see there was more world past the edge of the map. She let the river keep a
secret. She named the standing stones after her grandmother's cats and told no
one. Travelers crossed her valley slowly. They stopped often. They climbed
things. One of them is up there still, trying to work out why the lighthouse
blinks in sevens.

Nobody has ever explored a world because they were told to.

**If a world is fantastic, it's explored.**

---

## Three ways to read it

The sentence works three ways, and we use all three.

**As physics.** In this project the fable is a law of nature: fantastic worlds
*get* explored, the way ripe fruit gets eaten and lit windows get looked into.
So exploration is not a feature, and we will never build an "exploration
system." We build a world. Exploration is what happens to it.

**As a promise.** We will never make anyone explore. No markers, no chores, no
bribes, no homework. If the player crosses the hill, it is because the hill
made its case. Every step taken in this game is volunteered.

**As a verdict.** Read backwards, the fable becomes the tool we build with:
*if it isn't explored, it wasn't fantastic.* An unvisited corner is never the
player's failure. It is ours, at a known coordinate, and the fix is never a
reward sign planted in front of it. The fix is a better corner.

The fable is the dream. The contrapositive is the job.

## What "fantastic" is made of

"Fantastic" is not a mood; it is a material list, and it comes straight from
the brief: **beautiful, charming, detailed, alive, mysterious, wondrous.** Six
materials. Each one can be built, inspected, and gotten wrong in a specific
way — so each entry below says what the material is, what it is *for*, and
what it decays into when we get lazy.

("Interesting" is not on the list because it is the sum. A world is
interesting exactly when the six below are present.)

### Beauty

Beauty is not decoration. Beauty is **direction**: light tells you where to
look, and where you look becomes where you go. A beautiful world composes
itself into pictures from wherever the player happens to stand — the path
bends *because* the framing is better that way, the tower sits *there* because
that is where the evening sun catches it. Beauty is the world's first and
quietest gesture.

One hard rule inside it: every postcard is a place. If the player can see it
and want it, the player can walk to it and touch it.

**Decays into:** scenery — beauty you can photograph but not enter. A matte
painting is a broken promise with good lighting.

### Charm

Charm is **specificity plus warmth**, with a little imperfection left in on
purpose. A charming world has opinions. Its shutters hang slightly crooked.
Its cats have jobs. Its doors are painted colors somebody once argued about.
Nothing important is called "the ruins" — it is called *Wennow Cross*, and
the name is older than anyone's memory of why. A charming world makes small
jokes and does not explain them.

Charm is also restraint: it is sincere before it is quirky. Whimsy applied
evenly across a world is just beige with extra steps.

**Decays into:** quirk wallpaper — whimsy without sincerity, mascots without
lives.

### Detail

Detail is **attention, repaid**. Wherever the player chooses to look closer,
the world was already there first: the fence was mended twice in two
different styles, the bridge has initials in it, the third stair is worn
deeper than the rest. Every detail is evidence of a life being lived or a
history that happened — which means every detail is a small hypothesis the
player gets to form and test. Detail at every scale: lean in and there is
more; climb up and there is more.

**Decays into:** clutter — detail that answers no question and implies no
life. Density is not detail.

### Life

The world **does not wait for the player**. It has a clock of its own:
routines, weather, tides, seasons, arguments. The ferryman eats at noon
whether or not anyone is watching. The herons contest the good rock every
morning. Things happen off-screen and leave traces to find — feathers, ash,
a door that was closed yesterday. Missing a moment is proof the moment was
real.

(The gentleness clause, because life must not curdle into anxiety: the player
may miss a *moment*; the player never permanently loses a *truth*. Everything
essential can still be found, later, another way.)

**Decays into:** animatronics — motion without consequence, loops that reset,
a world that visibly reboots when you look away.

### Mystery

Small mysteries, honestly kept. A mystery is **a question the world raises
without asking it out loud**: a door with no keyhole, a song two villages
apart share one verse of, seven chimneys and six hearths. Mysteries are
*noticed, never assigned* — the moment a mystery becomes an objective it
stops being a mystery and becomes homework. Most mysteries resolve, and they
resolve into story or charm rather than loot. And a tithe of them never
resolve at all (see Law 9).

**Decays into:** tollbooths — riddles that gate progress and demand answers.
A mystery is an invitation, not a lock.

### Wonder

Wonder is the feeling of **the world exceeding your model of it** — the
moment the map in your head tears at the edge. It cannot be scheduled, only
prepared: it is engineered from contrast and withholding. The small door and
the vast room behind it. The long dark and the sudden green. The staircase
that keeps going *down*. Wonder needs silence before it and needs no fanfare
after it — the worst thing we could do to a wonder is have the game
congratulate the player for finding it.

**Decays into:** spectacle on a timer — awe as a scheduled deliverable,
fireworks at fixed intervals. Wonder repeated on demand is weather.

## The Ten Laws

The fable, made enforceable. When a design question comes up during the
build, it is answered here or it is answered wrong.

**1. The Law of the Horizon.**
*Everything you can see, you can reach; everything you can reach holds
something.* Every vista is a contract with two clauses. A visible peak with
no path is a lie; a reachable peak with nothing on it is a worse one.

**2. The Law of Gesture.**
*The world beckons; it never points.* All guidance is diegetic: light through
a gap, smoke over a ridge, birds all flying one way, a bell heard faintly, a
path worn by feet that came before, a pear tree in blossom above the hill.
The interface stays silent. There are no markers, pins, trails, or arrows —
if a place needs finding, the place itself must do the asking.

**3. The Law of Curiosity's Wage.**
*Exploration pays in world.* What you find by looking is a view, a shortcut,
a story, a song, a name, a room, a working thing — more world, always. Never
points, never currency, never a number going up. A prize for exploring is an
apology from a world that wasn't worth it, and we do not apologize in
advance.

**4. The Law of the Second Look.**
*Whatever is worth a glance is worth two.* First glance, beauty; second
glance, meaning. Places re-read differently once you know more, and time of
day, weather, and the world's own life mean a return visit is a new visit.
Nothing is finished by being seen once.

**5. The Law of Indifferent Life.**
*The world runs whether or not it is watched.* Routines continue, weather
crosses the map, the ferry keeps its schedule, and events leave traces
instead of waiting politely for an audience. The world's indifference is
warm, not cruel — it is the indifference of a place that was here before you
and is busy being itself. That is what makes being welcomed by it mean
something.

**6. The Law of the Only Key.**
*Knowledge opens everything.* Gates in this world yield to understanding —
a schedule learned, a pattern recognized, a name remembered, a path deduced —
never to collected tokens or accumulated numbers. Nothing essential hides
behind grinding. Everything essential hides behind noticing.

**7. The Law of Gentle Ground.**
*Curiosity is never punished.* No death for wandering, no fail state for
wondering. Falling into a cave is discovering a cave. Getting lost is not a
malfunction — *lost is a place here*, deliberately made, and it is nice
there, and it always leads somewhere. Where the world holds real danger, the
danger is legible, announced, and interesting in itself — never a trap
sprung on curiosity.

**8. The Law of Meant Things.**
*Everything is somewhere on purpose.* Every placed object can answer the
question "why are you here?" with a story — who left it, wore it, built it,
lost it. "Variety" is not an answer. "Filler" is a confession. If we cannot
answer for a thing, the thing goes. (Procedure may rough in terrain the way
an underpainting roughs in sky, but nothing ships that a deliberate eye
hasn't chosen and can't account for.)

**9. The Law of the Kept Mystery.**
*A tithe of mysteries stays unsolved. Forever. On purpose.* Roughly one in
ten of the world's questions has no answer anywhere in the game — the door
that never opens, the light nobody's found the source of, the seventh
chimney. A fully explained world is an exhausted world; wonder needs a
remainder. The unopenable door is load-bearing.

**10. The Law of the Filling Journal.**
*The game records; it never audits.* The player's map is drawn from where
they have actually been; their journal holds what they actually noticed, in
their terms. A journal **fills**; a checklist **empties** — the first frames
the world as life accumulating, the second frames it as work remaining.
There is no percentage, no X-of-Y, no completion screen. Done is a feeling,
not a number, and the game never contradicts the player about it.

## The Forbidden

What the fable rules out — each with its reason, because these are not
taboos, they are consequences.

- **Quest markers, compass pins, glowing trails.** They read the world so the
  player doesn't have to. The moment a marker exists, the player follows the
  marker and the world becomes the corridor between markers. This is the
  first mapmaker's valley.
- **Map icons before arrival; towers that unfog regions.** The map is memory,
  not inventory. A map that knows more than the player does makes the world
  secondhand.
- **Completion percentages and collectible counters.** They recast a world as
  a chore list and guarantee the last 20% is resentment.
- **XP, levels, loot, or currency as the point of a place.** Bribery. Paying
  people to explore is how you learn your world can't do it alone — and how
  you make sure they stop the day the paying stops.
- **Quest logs, objective trackers, and assigned wonder.** *(Amended — see
  Amendment I.)* What is forbidden is the apparatus of assignment: logs,
  trackers, markers, the word "objective," payment for compliance. The favor
  itself never was — a neighbor may ask you to carry bread up the hill,
  because a world where nobody needs anything is a diorama. The ask is
  refusable, the promise is remembered as a string tied to your journal, and
  no log ever exists.
- **Teleport-anywhere fast travel.** It deletes the world between places.
  Speed is earned as knowledge instead: shortcuts found, passes learned, the
  ferry caught — ways of moving that are themselves places and proofs that
  you know the world better than you did.
- **Minimap radar.** The screen is the map. Attention belongs on the world,
  not on a diagram of the world in the corner of the world.
- **Lore dumps and codex walls.** The world is not explained; it is
  *evident*. Books may exist — as objects, in rooms, with owners and
  marginalia — but history lives in worn stairs and mended fences, not in
  menus.
- **Expiring wonders.** Wonder with a deadline is a threat. Nothing fantastic
  is used to make the player afraid of being elsewhere.
- **The word "content."** Vocabulary shapes worlds. We do not say content,
  POI density, or engagement loop. We say *places, stories, secrets,
  moments.* A team that says "content" ships crates.

## The Covenant

What the world promises whoever enters it. Written to the player, kept by us.

1. **Your curiosity will never be punished.** The worst that follows a "what
   if" is a story to tell.
2. **Your attention will never be wasted.** Whatever we detailed, we meant.
   If you find yourself looking closer, there is something there.
3. **You will never be told where to go — and there will always be somewhere
   you want to.** If ever you stand still with nothing tugging at you, the
   world has failed you, not the reverse.
4. **If you wonder "can I…?", the answer is usually yes** — and was always
   considered. The hills have tops. The doors have insides. The lighthouse
   blinks in sevens for a reason, unless it's the other kind (see 6).
5. **You may miss moments; you will never lose truths.** The world runs
   without you, so you'll miss things — that's how you know it's real — but
   everything essential waits to be found another way.
6. **Some questions will never be answered.** This is a promise kept, not a
   promise broken. A world with a floor you can touch everywhere is a room.
7. **You are done when you feel done.** No number will ever appear to argue
   with you.

## The Tests

The fable as quality control. These are the rituals of the build — run them
constantly, and when one fails, fix the world, never the test.

1. **The Postcard Test.** Stop anywhere, screenshot without staging. Is it
   worth sending to someone? Every failure marks a place where beauty ran
   out.
2. **The Interrogation.** Point at any object: *"Why are you here?"* The
   answer must be a story. "Variety" fails. "Filler" is a confession, and the
   object is removed or given a life.
3. **The Silent Walk.** Watch someone play with the interface off and no
   prompts given. Note every coordinate where they stop moving *toward*
   something. The heatmap of abandoned walks is our bug tracker.
4. **The Rumor Test.** A day after playing, does the player tell someone
   something that sounds like a rumor? *"I swear the lighthouse blinks in a
   pattern."* A fantastic world generates rumors. A content world generates
   reviews.
5. **The Detour Test.** Ask a player to go from A to B. If they arrive
   without once being tempted sideways, the space between is dead, and the
   space between is the game.
6. **The Return Test.** Revisit any place after an hour spent elsewhere. Is
   anything different, or newly legible now that you know more? If a place
   can be finished, it isn't a place yet.

## What this commits us to

The philosophy's bill, itemized — what the build must now include, and what
it must now refuse, before a single hill is raised:

- **No marker system, objective system, XP system, or completion meter will
  ever be designed.** That engineering budget goes to light, weather, sound,
  routines, and memory instead. The absence is not a missing feature; it is
  the feature.
- **A guidance budget per place.** Anything that needs finding gets an
  authored gesture — its light, its sound, its birds, its worn path. Written
  down, per place, and playtested by the Silent Walk.
- **A mystery ledger.** Every question the world raises is tracked with
  where its answer lives — and a tenth of the entries just say **never**.
  The ledger keeps us honest in both directions: no accidental tollbooths,
  no accidentally exhausted world.
- **A world clock before a world.** Time, weather, routines, and schedules
  are the first system built, because life (Law 5) is a property of the
  simulation, not a coat of paint.
- **A journal that remembers, and never counts.** The player's record grows
  in their own terms — sketches of what they saw, names they learned —
  with no denominators anywhere in the code that renders it.
- **A "why" for every placed thing.** The build tracks each object's answer
  to the Interrogation. If the field is empty, the object doesn't ship.

One thing this document deliberately does not do is name the world or
describe it. Worlds described before their philosophy come out as brochures.
The philosophy ends here; the world begins next, and the world's name will be
found the way everything in it will be found — by going and looking.

## Amendments

The philosophy is a law code, not scripture. It can be amended — in writing,
with reasons, recorded here. It is never silently patched.

**Amendment I — Asks and Strings.** *(Added with the first population of
Wennow Vale.)* The original Forbidden list banned "fetch quests and
objectives," and half of that was right. The half that was wrong: a living
world contains people who need things, and a stranger you can ask a favor of
is one of the warmest objects a world can hold. What kills wonder was never
the favor — it is the apparatus: the log, the tracker, the exclamation mark,
the reward tab, the completion tick.

So, the doctrine of asks: people may ask things of the player, **face to
face, in their own words, at the right hour** — never through any interface.
An ask is refusable with a shake of the head, without penalty, and being
asked is itself a small honor. A promise accepted ties a **string** to the
player's journal — the vale's own custom for remembering — with a little
sketch of who asked; kept, it unties itself. Forgotten, it just stays,
patient, and nobody scolds. Consequences of kept promises are relational and
worldly — trust, doors, a name learned, a stripe of impossible color —
never numeric.

What remains forbidden forever: the quest log, the objective marker, the
tracker, timers on kindness, payment in numbers, and any ask that must be
accepted for the world to continue.

**Amendment II — The Rule of Three Temptations.** *(Added when the vale
was judged beautiful but not yet dense.)* Wonder needs room, but empty is
not the same as quiet. The standard, enforceable per screen: **any
screenful of world holds at least three noticeable things** — a doing, a
detail worth leaning at, a person or creature, a color spike, a sound with
a source. A quiet moor at dusk passes (heather, wind-lean, the stones); a
corridor of grass does not. Density is achieved with meant things, never
clutter: each of the three still answers the Interrogation. And a world's
cast now has two registers: the **majors** (few, knowable as neighbors)
and the **chorus** (many, one great line each) — a street with three
one-line strangers beats an empty street every time.

## Coda

Make the world fantastic, and the exploring takes care of itself.

Exploration is not the player's job. It is the world's fault.
